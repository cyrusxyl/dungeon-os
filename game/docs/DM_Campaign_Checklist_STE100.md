# DM Campaign Checklist
## Written in Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100 style)

**Note on this document:** This document uses short sentences and simple words. Each step gives one instruction. Use this checklist to write and run a good D&D campaign.

---

## 1. Before You Write the Story

**1.1** Write the campaign premise in one sentence.

**1.2** Write one hook. The hook must pull the players into the story at session 1.

**1.3** Set the tone of the campaign. Examples: heroic, dark, funny, scary.

**1.4** Decide the length of the campaign. A first campaign must have 6 to 10 sessions.

**1.5** Write 3 to 5 "world truths." A world truth is a fact that is always true in your world. Example: "Magic is rare."

**1.6** Do not write the full history of the world. Write only what you need for session 1.

**1.7** Write one starting location. Add 2 or 3 details about it.

---

## 2. Design the Villains

**2.1** Create 1 to 3 villains for the campaign.

**2.2** For each villain, write one goal. The goal must be clear.

**2.3** For each villain, write three actions the villain will take to reach the goal.

**2.4** Give each villain a way to escape capture. Do not let the players kill a main villain too early.

**2.5** Give each villain one clear trait. This trait helps players remember the villain.

**2.6** Do not stop the villain's actions when players are not present. The villain must act, even off-screen.

---

## 3. Design the Story Structure

**3.1** Do not write a fixed plot. Write situations instead.

**3.2** A situation has three parts: a location, a person, and a goal.

**3.3** For each major clue you want players to find, write three clues, not one. Players may miss the first clue. Players may ignore the second clue. Players may misunderstand the third clue.

**3.4** Place each clue in more than one possible location, when you can.

**3.5** If the players miss all clues, do not stop the story. Move the story forward in a new way.

**3.6** Do not force players onto one path. Let player choices change the story.

**3.7** Give the campaign a clear end point. Plan the final scene, but do not plan the exact path to it.

**3.8** End each session on a cliffhanger, when you can.

---

## 4. Balance the Three Types of Play

**4.1** The three types of play are: combat, exploration, and social interaction.

**4.2** Do not use only one type of play. Use all three types across the campaign.

**4.3** In each session, prepare at least one opportunity for combat.

**4.4** In each session, prepare at least one opportunity for exploration.

**4.5** In each session, prepare at least one opportunity for social interaction.

**4.6** Adjust the balance of the three types to match what your players enjoy.

---

## 5. Use Player Backstories

**5.1** Ask each player: "What people or places from your character's past do you want to see again?"

**5.2** Ask each player: "What unfinished problem does your character have?"

**5.3** Add each player's backstory people and places into the campaign.

**5.4** Do not remove a player's control over their own character. Do not kill a character's backstory person without the player's permission.

**5.5** Give equal backstory time to each player. Do not focus on one player's backstory for too long.

**5.6** Use a player's backstory to create a new problem. Do not use a backstory to end a character's reason to adventure.

---

## 6. Run Session Zero

**6.1** Hold a Session Zero meeting before session 1.

**6.2** At Session Zero, state the campaign tone, genre, and expected length.

**6.3** At Session Zero, state the rules you will use. State the source books you allow.

**6.4** At Session Zero, ask players how they want character death handled.

**6.5** At Session Zero, ask about safety limits. Use two categories: **hard limits** and **soft limits**.
  - A hard limit is a topic you must not include at all.
  - A soft limit is a topic you must include only with care.

**6.6** At Session Zero, agree on table rules. Cover phone use, side talk, and player conflict.

**6.7** At Session Zero, agree on the schedule. Cover session length, frequency, and the policy for missed sessions.

**6.8** At Session Zero, decide how the player characters know each other. Do not start session 1 with four strangers in a tavern.

**6.9** Write down all Session Zero agreements. Keep the notes. Use them again if a problem occurs later.

---

## 7. Prepare Each Session

**7.1** Before each session, review each player's character. Find one hook for each character.

**7.2** Write a strong start for the session. The strong start must happen in the first five minutes.

**7.3** Write a short list of possible scenes. Do not write full scenes. Write only bullet points.

**7.4** Write about ten clues or secrets for the session. Do not link each clue to one fixed location. This lets you place a clue anywhere the players go.

**7.5** For each location, write one sentence and one to three interesting details.

**7.6** For each NPC, write a name and one trait. Do not write a full history for minor NPCs.

**7.7** Prepare a loose list of monsters. Use this list to build encounters during the session, if needed.

**7.8** Prepare magic item rewards. Match some rewards to specific characters.

**7.9** Do not prepare content the players will not use. Prepare only what you need for the next session.

---

## 8. Run the Game: General Rules

**8.1** Your main duty as DM is to help all players have fun.

**8.2** Treat the game as a shared story, not a competition.

**8.3** Be fair. Apply rules the same way for all players.

**8.4** Stay flexible. Change your plan if the story needs it.

**8.5** Talk to your players about problems. Do not let problems build up.

**8.6** If you make a mistake, correct it and continue. Do not stop the game to fix small errors.

---

## 9. Decide When to Roll Dice

**9.1** Call for a dice roll only if both of these are true:
  - Success is possible.
  - Failure is possible.

**9.2** Call for a dice roll only if failure creates a real result in the story.

**9.3** Do not call for a roll if the task is easy. Tell the player the result directly.

**9.4** Do not call for a roll if the task is impossible. Tell the player it cannot succeed.

**9.5** After a failed roll, you may apply "success at a cost." This means the player succeeds, but a new problem appears.

---

## 10. Run Combat Well

**10.1** Give players a real choice on each combat turn. Do not let a turn become automatic.

**10.2** End a combat encounter when the real choices are used up. Do not extend a fight past this point.

**10.3** Add interactive terrain to combat locations. Examples: pits, fire, cover, high ground.

**10.4** Warn players about a dangerous threat before combat starts, when possible.

**10.5** Describe the result of an action only after you resolve the dice roll.

**10.6** Prepare your monster stat blocks before the session. This will make combat faster.

---

## 11. Manage Session Pacing

**11.1** Watch for signs that players are bored. Signs include: phone use, side talk, and long silence.

**11.2** If a player is quiet, ask them a direct question. Example: "What is your character thinking now?"

**11.3** If the group is stuck in planning, add a new event to the scene. Example: a guard arrives.

**11.4** Move the spotlight between players often. Do not let one player speak for too long.

**11.5** Keep individual scenes short.

---

## 12. Manage the Table

**12.1** Give each player time in the spotlight during each session.

**12.2** If a player disrupts the game, talk to them outside of the session.

**12.3** If a player takes your DM choices personally, talk to them outside of the game.

**12.4** Treat all players with respect. Do not mock a player's choice.

---

## 13. Use Improvisation

**13.1** When a player tries a new action, say "yes" when you can. Then add a new detail.

**13.2** Do not say "no" to a player action without a clear reason.

**13.3** If you must say no, offer a different path instead. This is called "yes, but" or "no, and."

**13.4** Write down names and details you invent during a session. Use them again later.

---

## 14. Give Meaningful Consequences

**14.1** Every failed roll must have a real consequence.

**14.2** A consequence must come from the character's action or inaction.

**14.3** Avoid consequences with no purpose. A consequence must move the story forward or affect a character.

**14.4** After a session, show the players the result of an earlier choice, when it fits the story.

---

## 15. Common Mistakes: Do Not Do These

**15.1** Do not remove player choice to force your planned story. This is called railroading.

**15.2** Do not over-prepare content. Do not build more than the group can use.

**15.3** Do not let players use knowledge their character does not have. This is called metagaming.

**15.4** Do not use only one type of play for the whole campaign.

**15.5** Do not call for a dice roll with no stakes.

**15.6** Do not let one player dominate every scene.

**15.7** Do not kill a recurring villain in an early encounter.

**15.8** Do not compare your table to a professional stream. Your table is different.

---

## 16. After Each Session

**16.1** Write a short recap of the session's events.

**16.2** At the start of the next session, read the recap to the players.

**16.3** Ask the players for feedback.

**16.4** Adjust your prepared material to match the players' choices and interests.

---

## Quick Reference: Minimum Steps for a First Campaign

1. Write the premise, hook, tone, and length. (Section 1)
2. Design 1 to 3 villains with clear goals. (Section 2)
3. Prepare situations, not plots. Use the three-clue rule. (Section 3)
4. Balance combat, exploration, and social play. (Section 4)
5. Collect and use player backstories. (Section 5)
6. Run Session Zero and write down the agreements. (Section 6)
7. Prepare each session with the eight-step method. (Section 7)
8. Follow the dice-roll rule during play. (Section 9)
9. Watch for common mistakes and avoid them. (Section 15)
10. Recap and collect feedback after each session. (Section 16)
